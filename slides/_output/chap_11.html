Chapter 11: The Life Table and Life Expectancy

Life tables

The distribution of lifetime for a population is often summarised in a life table.

This is English Life Tables No. 17: 2010 to 2012, a life table for England.

Life table quantities \(\ell_x\), \(q_x\) and \(d_x\) (Gap on page 120)

The life table summarises the distribution of a lifetime variable by presenting the function \(\ell_x\) for a set of discrete values \(x\).

The number \(\ell_0\) is called the radix for the table, and

\[ \begin{aligned} \ell_x&= \mathbb{E}[\mbox{number of survivors to exact age } x\\ &\qquad \mbox{ from an initial population of size }\ell_0]\\ &=\ell_0 \mathbb{P}(T>x)\\ &=\ell_0 S_T(x) \end{aligned} \]

So \(\ell_x/\ell_0\) is simply the survival function \(S_T(x)\) or \({}_x p_0\).

  • The radix is usually a power of 10, such as \(10\,000\) or \(100\,000\)
  • The \(x\) values are usually evenly spaced but need not be every year, for example every 5 years or every 10 years.

The life table quantity \(q_x\) (Gap on pages 120)

The life table usually contains a column with \(q_x\) values.

If the gap between the ages \(x\) is 1 year, then \(q_x\) is exactly as defined in Section 10.5, that is

\[ \begin{aligned} q_x=\mathbb{P}(T\le x+1|T>x)&= 1-S_T(1|x)\\[2pt] &=1-\frac{S_T(x+1)}{S_T(x)} \end{aligned} \]

It follows that there is a simple relationship between \(\{\ell_x\}\) and \(\{q_x\}\), as \[ q_x\;\;=\;\;1-\frac{S_T(x+1)}{S_T(x)}\;\;=\;\;1-\frac{\ell_{x+1}}{\ell_x} \] and \[ \ell_{x+1}\;=\;\ell_x(1-q_x)\;\;=\;\;\ell_0\prod_{k=0}^{x}(1-q_k) \]

If the gap between the \(x\) values is \(g>1\) year, then the \(q_x\) should be replaced by \({}_gq_x\).

The life table quantity \(d_x\) (Gap on page 121)

The life table usually contains a column with \(d_x\) values.

\[ \begin{aligned} d_x&= \mathbb{E}[\mbox{number of deaths in } [x,x+1) \mbox{ from an initial population of size }\ell_0]\\ &=\ell_0 P(x\le T<x+1)\\ &=\ell_0 [S_T(x)-S_T(x+1)]\\ &=\ell_x-\ell_{x+1}. \end{aligned} \]

Life expectancy (Gap on page 122)

The complete life expectancy at age \(x\), \(\overset{\circ}{e}_{x}\) is defined as the expected future lifetime given survival to age \(x\), \[ \overset{\circ}{e}_x\;=\;\mathbb{E}(T-x\,|\,T>x) \]

The curtate life expectancy at age \(x\), \(e_{x}\) is defined as the expected future lifetime in completed years given survival to age \(x\) (usually an integer),

\[ \begin{aligned} e_x&=\mathbb{E}([T-x]\,|\,T>x)\\ &=\sum_{k=0}^\infty k \mathbb{P}(x+k\le T< x+k+1\,|\,T>x)\\ &=\sum_{k=0}^\infty k [S_T(k|x)-S_T(k+1|x)]\\ &=\sum_{k=0}^\infty k \frac{S_T(x+k)-S_T(x+k+1)}{S_T(x)} \end{aligned} \]

Obtaining \(e_x\) from the life table (Gap on page 123)

If \(x\) is an integer, then

\[ \begin{aligned} e_x&=\sum_{k=0}^\infty k \frac{S_T(x+k)-S_T(x+k+1)}{S_T(x)}\\ &=\sum_{k=0}^\infty k \frac{\ell_{x+k}-\ell_{x+k+1}}{\ell_x}\\ &=\frac{1}{\ell_x}\sum_{k=0}^\infty k (\ell_{x+k}-\ell_{x+k+1})\\ &= \frac{1}{\ell_x}\sum_{k=1}^\infty \ell_{x+k} \end{aligned} \]

In practice, we only need to sum up until \(\ell_{x+k}\approx 0\) (typically around \(x+k=120\)).

Obtaining \(\overset{\circ}{e}_x\) from \(e_x\) (Gap on page 124)

The complete expectation of life \(\overset{\circ}{e}_x\) includes fractional parts of a year between an individuals last birthday (the death age for the purposes of calculation of \(e_x\)) and the actual date of death. Hence \[ e_x \le \overset{\circ}{e}_x < e_x+1. \]

If we make the assumption that, in every age interval \([x,x+1)\), deaths are uniformly distributed, then we have \[ \overset{\circ}{e}_x = e_x+\frac{1}{2}. \] This gives a reasonable approximation to \(\overset{\circ}{e}_x\) which can then be computed using life table data.

However, in cases where the \(x\) values are not evenly spaced, or when the assumption that deaths are evenly distributed over \([x,x+1)\) is unreasonable (for example in the first year of life) then an alternative approach is required to compute \(\overset{\circ}{e}_x\).

Obtaining \(\overset{\circ}{e}_x\) from irregular life table (Gap on page 124)

We have

\[ \begin{aligned} \overset{\circ}{e}_x= E(T-x|T>x)&=\int_0^\infty S_T(t|x)\mathrm{d}t\\ &=\int_0^\infty \frac{S_T(x+t)}{S_T(x)}\mathrm{d}t\\ &=\frac{1}{\ell_x}\int_0^\infty \ell_0 S_T(x+t)\mathrm{d}t\\ &=\frac{1}{\ell_x}\int_x^\infty \ell_0 S_T(t)\mathrm{d}t\\ &=\frac{1}{\ell_x}\sum_{i=1} \int_{x_{i-1}}^{x_i} \ell_0 S_T(t)\mathrm{d}t \end{aligned} \]

where \(x=x_0<x_1<x_2\cdots\) are the ages \(\ge x\) represented in the life table.

Expectation of life in an interval (Gap on page 125)

Define \({}_{s}L_x\) to be the expected total years lived between ages \(x\) and \(x+s\) by an initial population of size \(\ell_0\). Then \[ {}_{s}L_x\;\;=\;\; {}_{\infty}L_x-{}_{\infty}L_{x+s} \]

where \[ {}_{\infty}L_x\;\;=\;\; \ell_0 \mathbb{P}(T>x) \overset{\circ}{e}_x\;\;=\;\; \ell_0 \frac{\ell_x}{\ell_0}\frac{1}{\ell_x}\int_x^\infty \ell_0 S_T(t)\mathrm{d}t\;\;=\;\; \int_x^\infty \ell_0 S_T(t)\mathrm{d}t. \]

Therefore, \[ {}_{s}L_x\;\;=\;\; \int_x^{x+s} \ell_0 S_T(t)\mathrm{d}t \]

The quantity \({}_{\infty}L_x\), the expected total years lived after age \(x\) by an initial population of size \(\ell_0\), is usually denoted by \(T_x\).

Life expectancy using \(L_x\) and \(T_x\) (Gap on page 126)

It follows from Section 11.3.3 and 11.3.4 that life expectancy at birth is

\[ \begin{aligned} \overset{\circ}{e}_x&=\frac{1}{\ell_x}\int_x^\infty \ell_0 S_T(t)\mathrm{d}t\;\;=\;\;\frac{T_x}{\ell_x}\\ &=\frac{1}{\ell_x}\sum_{i=1} \int_{x_{i-1}}^{x_i} \ell_0 S_T(t)\mathrm{d}t\;\;=\;\; \frac{1}{\ell_x}\sum_{i=1} {}_{x_{i}-x_{i-1}}L_{x_{i-1}} \end{aligned} \]

When \(t=1\), \({}_{t}L_x\) is simply denoted by \(L_x\).

Therefore, for a life table with annual spacing, \(x_0=x\), \(x_1=x+1,\ldots\), we have \[ \overset{\circ}{e}_x\;\;=\;\;\frac{T_x}{\ell_x}\;\;=\;\; \frac{1}{\ell_x}\sum_{t=0} L_{x+t} \]

Life tables often present values of \(T_x\) and (or) \(L_x\).

The trapezium rule approximation (Gap on page 127)

To obtain \(\overset{\circ}{e}_x\), we need to approximate \({}_{x_{i}-x_{i-1}}L_{x_{i-1}}=\int_{x_{i-1}}^{x_i} \ell_0 S_T(t)\mathrm{d}t\) where the only relevant values of the integrand that we know are at the endpoints \[ \ell_0 S_T(x_{i-1})=\ell_{x_{i-1}}\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad\ell_0 S_T(x_i)=\ell_{x_i} \]

We use the trapezium rule approximation.

x_left <- 1.5
x_right <- 3.5
y_left <- exp(-x_left)
y_right <- exp(-x_right)

The trapezium rule approximation (Gap on page 127)

\[ {}_{x_{i}-x_{i-1}}L_{x_{i-1}}\;\;=\;\;\int_{x_{i-1}}^{x_i} \ell_0 S_T(t)\mathrm{d}t\;\;\approx\;\;(x_i-x_{i-1})\frac{\ell_{x_i}+\ell_{x_{i-1}}}{2} \] so, when \(x_i-x_{i-1}=1\) for all \(i\), we have \(L_x\approx(\ell_{x+1}+\ell_x)/2\) which gives the expression in Section 11.3.2 when substituted into \(\overset{\circ}{e}_x=\frac{1}{\ell_x}\sum_{t=0} L_{x+t}\).

Concluding remarks: Period life tables

The life table English Life Tables No. 17: 2010 to 2012 in Section 11.1 is a period life table.

That means that the table was constructed by estimating \(q_x\) from mortality data over a particular period in time (here 2010-2012).

  • \(q_{21}\) is estimated using individuals born around 1990,
  • \(q_{81}\) is estimated using individuals born around 1930, etc.

So the table does not actually describe the distribution of lifetime of any actual individual or group of individuals.

  • Individuals born around 1990 will expect to experience a different (lower?) \(q_{81}\) in 2071.
  • Individuals born around 1930 experienced a higher \(q_{21}\) in 1951.

Concluding remarks: Cohort life tables

A life table describing the mortality experience of a particular population, as it develops over time is called a cohort life table.

A cohort is the name given to a population all born at or around the same time (typically in the same calendar year).

The cohort life table estimates \(q_x\) based on the mortality experience of the cohort at that age.

The problem with a cohort life table is that it cannot be completed until the cohort have died out, by which point it is only really of interest to historians!
